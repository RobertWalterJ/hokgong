# Pedagogy audit — is this rigorous against the literature?

*20 September 2026. Robert asked: "Is the teaching and progression module
rigorous yet, and is it up to performing against the literature for what is
needed for effective second language learning?"*

Short answer: **the part it does, it does well and on good evidence. It is
about one quarter of a course, and the missing three quarters are the parts
that turn knowledge into speech.**

## The framework

The standard way to audit a language course is Paul Nation's **four strands**
(*Learning Vocabulary in Another Language*, CUP; "The Four Strands", *IJLLT*
2007). A balanced course gives roughly **equal time** to:

1. **Meaning-focused input** — listening and reading you mostly understand already.
2. **Meaning-focused output** — speaking and writing to communicate something real.
3. **Language-focused learning** — deliberate study: vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation.
4. **Fluency development** — using what you already know, faster, with nothing new in it.

Here is where Hok Gong sits.

| strand | what the app does | share of a session |
|---|---|---|
| Language-focused learning | 6,000 words on spaced retrieval, ten gated stages, ten grammar points, tone drills, confusable-word notes | **~85%** |
| Meaning-focused input | 300 recorded sentences chosen for 85%+ known words, and an example sentence on every answer card | ~12% |
| Meaning-focused output | "Say this in Cantonese", now checkable by speech recognition | ~3% |
| Fluency development | **nothing** | 0% |

That is a vocabulary-and-grammar trainer, which is what it set out to be, and
it is not yet a course.

## What is well supported

These are the parts I would defend against the literature without hedging.

- **Retrieval practice over re-reading.** Roediger & Karpicke (2006); testing
  produces better retention than restudy, and the effect is large.
- **Spacing, with expanding intervals.** Cepeda et al. (2006) meta-analysis of
  254 studies: distributed practice beats massed, and the optimal gap grows with
  the retention interval you want. The scheduler is a simple SM-2 variant doing
  exactly this, and `build/test-schedule.mjs` plays 120 days to prove it holds.
- **No repeats within a session.** Same literature: within-session repetition
  inflates immediate performance and does little for retention. The app forbids
  it and enforces a four-hour cool-down on top.
- **Frequency-ordered vocabulary with measured coverage.** Nation's coverage
  method, computed on this corpus rather than borrowed from English.
- **Mastery before progression.** Bloom's mastery learning: the gate per stage
  is exactly this, and it is the change made today.
- **Interleaving skills** rather than blocking one kind of question.
- **Immediate feedback with the evidence attached** — the answer card shows the
  word, its reading, an example sentence and the source.

## What is missing, in the order it costs the most

### 1. Fluency development — nothing at all (strand 4)

Knowing 300 words slowly is not the same as using 100 words quickly. The
literature is specific: fluency comes from **repeated use of already-known
material under mild time pressure**, e.g. Nation's 4/3/2 technique.

The complication here is real: **no timers**, because of dyslexia, is a
standing rule in this workspace and it is not up for negotiation. So fluency
has to be built without a clock — repetition and familiarity rather than speed
under pressure. A "these are all words you know" round, run several times over
a week, gets most of the benefit without a countdown anywhere.

### 2. Meaning-focused output — almost nothing (strand 2)

Saying a word into a phone is not communication. What the literature asks for
is output with **communicative intent** and, ideally, a listener who responds.
The app cannot be that listener. What it *can* do is prepare the ground: give
you three things to try saying to your wife tonight, drawn from the stage you
are on, and ask tomorrow whether you said them. That is a real intervention —
it is the "pushed output" hypothesis (Swain) done by proxy.

### 3. Formulaic language — the app teaches words, conversation runs on chunks

Wray, *Formulaic Language and the Lexicon* (2002), and a large literature
since: fluent speech is built largely of stored multi-word units, not words
assembled from scratch. The corpus has these — 唔好意思, 係咪呀, 唔使客氣 — and
the app currently treats them as vocabulary items among six thousand others.
They deserve their own stream.

### 4. One voice, for tone training

High-variability phonetic training — multiple talkers, immediate feedback —
is the established method for learning a tone system as an adult (Logan, Lively
& Pisoni 1991 for /r/–/l/; Wang, Spence, Jongman & Sereno 2003 for Mandarin
tones, where it produced ~21% improvement that transferred to new voices).
Hok Gong has **one** speaker, in the Tatoeba recordings, and a synthetic voice.
Common Voice zh-HK (CC0) would fix this and is a download away.

### 5. The coverage numbers need honest interpretation

The app reports that the first 1,000 words cover 90.7% of recorded
conversation. True, and it should not be read as "you will follow 90% of a
conversation". Hu & Nation (2000) put unassisted reading comprehension at
**98%** coverage; van Zeeland & Schmitt (2013) put adequate listening
comprehension at around **95%**, which on this corpus is about **2,000 words**.
The Progress screen says coverage is not comprehension, but it could say what
the thresholds actually are.

### 6. Receptive before productive is right; the app should say which it is measuring

Nation's distinction: receptive knowledge (you understand it) precedes
productive (you can produce it), and they need different practice. The app has
both — 45% of questions are production — but until today the production
questions were **self-marked**, which makes them a flashcard rather than a
measurement. Speech recognition now marks them where the browser supports it;
where it does not, the app records that the answer was self-rated so the
Progress figures can distinguish the two.

## The verdict

**Rigorous as far as it goes, and about a quarter of a course.** If the only
thing you ever did was this app, the literature predicts what the persona
simulation already predicts: a solid receptive vocabulary, a good ear for
tones, correct grammar patterns — and halting speech, because nothing here
practises speaking under real conditions.

The good news is that you already have strands 1, 2 and 4 in your life: a
Cantonese wife, a bilingual toddler, mm.millmilk, family around a table. The
honest framing is that **this app is the language-focused strand of a course
whose other three strands are your household** — and the app should be built to
feed them, rather than pretending to replace them.

## What I would build next, in order

1. **Tonight's three phrases** — from your current stage, to try on a real
   person, with a check-back tomorrow. (Strand 2, cheap, high value.)
2. **An easy round** — only words you already know, no new material, run often.
   (Strand 4, no clock.)
3. **Chunks as their own stream** — the multi-word units the corpus already
   contains, taught whole.
4. **Narrow listening** — several recorded sentences on one topic, replayed,
   at the level you can nearly follow. (Strand 1.)
5. **Common Voice zh-HK** for many-voice tone training.
6. **Coverage thresholds** stated on the Progress screen: 95% for listening,
   98% for unassisted comprehension, and where you are against them.
