Design and evidence · 20 September 2026

Learning 廣東話, measured

Can the learning engine behind Palimpsest be turned to Cantonese — reading, listening and speaking — in a way that is scientifically defensible and honest about progress? Yes. Here is the method, the evidence behind it, what has already been built, and what it can't do.

The short answer

Three things carry over unchanged: retrieval practice (being asked, not shown), spacing (asked again as you are about to forget), and honest measurement (what you answered, not what you were exposed to). All three are among the best-supported findings in the study of memory, and vocabulary is the material they were most often tested on.

Three things are new: audio, speaking, and a frequency-ordered core. The corpus work is done: a word list built from recorded Hong Kong conversation, joined to dictionaries, with 1,784 sentences recorded by a human speaker.

What one thousand words is worth

Counted, not assumed. Every word in 58 transcripts of recorded Hong Kong conversation was ranked by how often people actually said it, then the running coverage was computed.

A caution on the coverage figure: 90.5% of words is not 90.5% of understanding — the missing one word in ten is usually the content word carrying the point. It is a measure of the ground covered, not of comprehension.

The evidence

  • Retrieval beats review — and dropping items is what kills recall Karpicke & Roediger (2008, Science) taught foreign-language word pairs. Items kept in testing were recalled about 80% a week later; items dropped from testing once learnt, but still restudied, fell to about 35%. The engine's rule — a word stays in rotation until you get it right after a real gap — comes straight from this.
  • Spacing, with gaps that grow Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006) reviewed 254 studies: spaced practice beat massed practice, and the advantage grew with how long you needed to remember. Cepeda et al. (2008) put numbers on the gap: to remember for months, review at intervals of roughly a tenth to a fifth of that period. Pimsleur's "graduated interval recall" (1967) was the same idea, hard-coded into a tape.
  • Why you ran out of Pimsleur Pimsleur fixes the schedule to the recording. Every learner gets the same intervals, and when the tapes end, the schedule ends. An adaptive scheduler sets each word's interval from your answers, and never runs out while the corpus has words left.
  • Tones can be trained, and the training transfers Wang, Spence, Jongman & Sereno (1999) trained English speakers on Mandarin tones with many different voices ("high-variability" training): identification rose about 21 percentage points, held six months later, and transferred to new voices, new words, and to their own pronunciation. Cantonese-specific evidence is thinner, and Cantonese is harder — six tones, with T2/T5 and T3/T6 close enough that learners and even some speakers merge them.
  • Pronunciation teaching works when it is explicit and gives feedback Lee, Jang & Plonsky (2015) meta-analysed 86 studies of pronunciation instruction and found a large average improvement, bigger when feedback was given. Speech recognition as a feedback channel is weaker evidence, and for Cantonese the recognisers are less reliable than for Mandarin; it is used here as a rough check plus your own judgement, never as a grade.
  • Input matters, but input alone is slow Watching Peppa Pig in Cantonese is genuinely useful — narrow, repetitive, child-directed input is close to ideal listening material. What it lacks is retrieval: you recognise without being asked to produce. The design pairs them, with words drawn from what you are actually hearing at home.

Effect sizes come from controlled studies, most of them short. None of them promise a result from an app: what they support is that this method is the right one to build, and that its progress should be measured rather than assumed.

The four skills, and how each is tested

  • ListeningA real recorded sentence plays; you choose what it means, or type what you heard. Audio first, text second — the opposite of most apps. 1,784 recorded sentences, 343 of them within the first 300 words.
  • TonesMinimal pairs drawn from words you are learning, not nonsense syllables: 係 hai6 against 喺 hai2, 噉 gam2 against 咁 gam3. Which tone did you hear? A confusion matrix shows which pairs you actually mix up.
  • SpeakingYou see a prompt or hear a model, then record. The phone's recogniser transcribes it, and the app scores the match syllable by syllable. Its verdict is advisory — the real check is whether your wife understands you, which the app asks you to report.
  • ReadingTraditional characters as used in Hong Kong, with Jyutping that you can turn off once you no longer need it. Sentence reading, not single characters, because a character's meaning depends on its pronunciation and its neighbours.

What progress will mean

Everything shown is something you did, with the uncertainty stated. Nothing is a streak or a badge.

What this can't do, and what it needs from you

  • It is not a class or a tutor. It builds vocabulary, listening and tone discrimination, and gives speaking practice a machine can check only roughly. Conversation with a person is a different skill and this doesn't replace it.
  • The corpus is from 1997–98. Everyday grammar and conversation hold up; anything about technology or money may be dated.
  • About a third of the commonest words have no good English gloss in the open dictionaries — the gloss on file belongs to another reading or to literary Chinese (都 given as "city" when it means "also"). Those need a Cantonese speaker to check. Your wife is the right reviewer, and the app will show exactly which words need her, a few at a time.
  • Speech recognition for Cantonese is imperfect. A poor score may be the recogniser's fault, and the app will say so rather than marking you down.
  • Your son is the best reason to start with the right words. A "what he hears" list — food, body, animals, bath, sleep, the Peppa Pig vocabulary — can be built and put first, so that what you practise is what you can use at home that day.

Build order

  1. Core loop, listening first. The 300 most frequent spoken words, recorded sentences, the scheduler already built and tested (no repeats in a session, a cool-down, growth shown from what you answered).
  2. Tones. Minimal-pair drills from the words you are learning, with the confusion matrix.
  3. Speaking. Record, compare, self-report; shadowing against the recorded sentences.
  4. Reading. Sentences with Jyutping that can be switched off; characters by frequency.
  5. Your own material. Words your son is hearing; the vocabulary from your Complete Cantonese book, typed in and checked against the dictionaries.
  6. Review pass with a speaker. The words whose glosses are uncertain, in small batches.
